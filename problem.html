The data landlords
rely on is broken

Credit scores, references, and one-off checks were never designed to answer the question you actually need answered: how does this person behave across a real tenancy?

The gap

Every tool freezes
at move-in

The UK landlord toolset was built for a simpler world. Credit checks, landlord references, and tenant passports all tell you who someone was at the moment they applied. None of them tell you what happens next.

The best predictor of how a tenant will behave in your property is how they've behaved in every property before it — not who they presented as on a Saturday afternoon viewing.

That data has always existed. It just hasn't been collected, verified, or made accessible. Until now, every landlord has been making a £50,000+ decision with a CV and a credit score.

Section 21 changed everything. The no-fault eviction route is gone. The cost of a wrong decision has never been higher — and the tools landlords are using haven't changed at all.

Four failure modes

Why the existing tools
don't work

01

Credit scores miss the point entirely

A credit score was designed to predict whether someone will repay a loan — not whether they'll pay rent on time. Most rent payments never appear on a credit file at all. A tenant with five years of perfect rent payments and no other debt can still score 480 because of a missed mobile phone bill from 2019.

The score isn't measuring the wrong thing poorly — it's measuring a completely different thing altogether. A high credit score tells you someone is good at managing debt products. It tells you almost nothing about whether they'll pay their rent on the 1st of every month for the next two years.

The gap
Only 3 of the UK's 4.6 million private renters have their rent payments reported to credit agencies — usually only when they actively opt in via a third-party service. The other 4.597 million are invisible.
What this means for you
A tenant can have paid every landlord they've ever had on time, every month, for a decade — and a standard credit check will give you no indication of this whatsoever.
02

References are biased by design

The mechanics of tenant references create a structural bias toward positive feedback. The previous landlord wants the tenant to leave — and if they give a bad reference, they may face a dispute, legal exposure, or an angry tenant who simply refuses to vacate.

So most landlord references say nothing meaningful. They confirm dates of tenancy, describe the tenant as "reliable" and "clean," and hope the incoming landlord doesn't read between the lines. By the time a reference is being checked, the previous landlord wants it to succeed.

The bias problem
A landlord who gives an honest negative reference risks a complaint or legal challenge. Most choose the path of least resistance: say nothing useful and let the problem move somewhere else.
What this means for you
References are almost never independent, always unverified, and written by someone whose incentives are misaligned with yours. You're making a trust decision based on a letter written by someone who benefits from you saying yes.
03

One-off checks freeze in time

Even the best tenant referencing products — Goodlord, Canopy, Homeppl — all share a structural problem: they check someone at one moment in time and then stop. The report you get on the day of move-in is the only report you'll ever get.

But the behaviour that matters — whether rent arrives on the 1st, whether issues get reported, whether the property is respected — unfolds over months and years. Nothing in a move-in check tells you how someone behaves in month seven when their circumstances change.

The snapshot problem
Someone can pass every check on the day they move in and become your most difficult tenant six months later when their job situation changes. The check captured a moment, not a pattern.
What this means for you
You're making a multi-year commitment based on a point-in-time snapshot. No bank would lend that way. No insurer would underwrite that way. But landlords have had no alternative.
04

Section 21 just raised the stakes

Before May 2026, a landlord could end a tenancy without giving any reason — Section 21 was the safety net. If a tenant turned out to be a problem, you could issue a no-fault notice and reclaim the property in two months.

That safety net no longer exists. Under the Renters' Rights Act 2024, every eviction now requires grounds. Court proceedings for non-payment of rent are estimated to take 11 months on average — during which a non-paying tenant remains in your property and you continue to owe a mortgage.

What changed in 2026
Section 21 'no-fault' evictions were abolished under the Renters' Rights Act 2024. Landlords must now demonstrate legal grounds for every possession — a process that takes an average of 11 months in court.
The new maths
11 months of missed rent at £1,200/month = £13,200 in lost income, plus an estimated £3,000–£6,000 in legal costs. Better screening isn't a nice-to-have any more. It's financial self-defence.

Legislative context

How the law changed
the equation

The Renters' Rights Act 2024 was the most significant shift in UK landlord law in a generation. The removal of Section 21 wasn't just a policy change — it fundamentally altered the risk profile of every tenancy agreement in England.

For the first time, the quality of your tenant screening has a direct and measurable impact on your exposure. The tools available haven't kept pace with the risk.
